Selectivity is an interfacial question.

My CO₂ reduction research asked how the ionomer surrounding a Cu catalyst affects product selectivity—and how that interface changes during extended operation.

Why is this research needed?

The electrochemical CO₂ reduction reaction (CO₂RR) converts CO₂ into valuable chemicals. Among metal catalysts, copper is distinctive in its ability to produce substantial amounts of multicarbon (C₂+) products.

Previous studies have reported that coatings of perfluorosulfonic acid (PFSA) ionomers, such as Nafion, can enhance C₂+ production. However, fuel-cell research has shown that hydroxyl radicals (•OH) can degrade PFSA membranes, including Nafion. Hydroxyl-radical generation has also been reported during CO₂RR.

Together, these findings motivate a question: could hydroxyl radicals generated during CO₂RR degrade the PFSA coating and affect the selectivity and stability of the catalyst–ionomer interface?

Results

Balancing Cu thickness and Nafion loading

Our team compared Cu thicknesses of 150, 300, and 450 nm, followed by Nafion loadings of 28.75, 86.25, and 258.75 μg cm⁻². Among the conditions tested, 300 nm Cu with 86.25 μg cm⁻² Nafion provided the most favorable C₂+ selectivity. Excess Nafion loading increased charge-transfer resistance and favored hydrogen evolution. We selected this Cu–Nafion combination on PTFE at −3.2 V vs. Ag/AgCl for extended-operation experiments.

Short-term performance did not guarantee durability

No clear deterioration was observed between 30 minutes and 1 hour. During longer operation, however, gas-phase C₂+ Faradaic efficiency (FE) consistently decreased while H₂ FE increased.

Investigating the origin of performance loss

We used coumarin-based radical probing and post-operation XPS to investigate possible Nafion degradation. The report describes spectral features interpreted as consistent with radical formation and changes in the ionomer’s sulfur-containing groups. These observations motivate further investigation, but do not establish that hydroxyl-radical-induced Nafion degradation caused the performance loss.

The main finding is a clear loss of C₂+ selectivity during extended operation. Establishing its mechanism will require more sensitive radical detection, such as EPR, and systematic before-and-after XPS measurements with additional samples.
